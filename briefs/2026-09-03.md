## Today's vocabulary

**Term of the day: eval harness.** The scaffolding that runs a model against a benchmark: how the prompt is constructed, how many actions are allowed, and whether reasoning state carries between requests. ARC Prize published both harness results for Astra today, and the gap was 62.7% versus 99.9%. Use it to attack any single benchmark number: a score without its harness is not a measurement, and the vendor picks the harness.

**Word of the day: Part 555 exemption.** The NHTSA process that lets a manufacturer sell up to 2,500 vehicles a year that do not meet a federal motor vehicle safety standard, which is the route a car with no steering wheel would normally take. No public Part 555 grant for Cybercab turned up in a search of NHTSA and Federal Register records today, which implies Tesla is self-certifying compliance instead. Zoox used the exemption route.

## Need to know

### 1. Tesla launched Cybercab in Austin with no steering wheel and no federal exemption on record

**What happened.** Tesla deployed what Reuters described as dozens of Cybercabs, built without steering wheels or pedals, onto Austin streets on 3 September. Tesla says its unsupervised robotaxi fleet now exceeds 200 vehicles across six cities in Texas and Florida. Waymo runs more than 4,000 across 14 cities. Tesla shares rose more than 5%. A Tesla SEC exhibit filed 1 August puts installed Cybercab tooling capacity at Gigafactory Texas above 125,000 units a year, with volume production described as on schedule for 2026; the Texas Motor Carrier Credentialing System showed 45 Cybercab units registered as of 31 August.

**Why it matters.** The binding constraint is now legal, not technical. A vehicle with no steering wheel fails at least one federal motor vehicle safety standard written around a human driver, and the normal route is a NHTSA Part 555 exemption capped at 2,500 units a year. No such grant for Cybercab appears in the public record, which means Tesla is asserting compliance itself. If that holds, Tesla scales past the 2,500-unit cap that binds Zoox and the 125,000-unit tooling number becomes reachable. If NHTSA disagrees, the fleet stops. Several NHTSA investigations into Tesla's self-driving software, covering crashes in poor visibility and red-light violations, are still open.

**So what for me.** This is the fleet electrification leg of the convergence thesis getting a date. Two hundred vehicles is noise for a utility forecast; 125,000 a year is not. Track the registration count in the Texas credentialing system as the real number, because it is verifiable and the fleet claims are not.

**Go deeper.** The 1 August Tesla 8-K exhibit, ten minutes. Question to hold: does Tesla anywhere state a regulatory basis for operating a vehicle without manual controls, or does the filing simply not address it? The absence is the finding.

**Second-order read, who loses.** Zoox, which spent years securing an exemption capped at 2,500 vehicles. If self-certification works, the exemption process was a self-imposed constraint and the compliance moat around it was worth nothing.

**Source.** [Reuters via Yahoo, 3 Sep 2026, Tier 2](https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/musk-launches-steering-wheel-free-205949871.html), `confirmed` for the launch and the share move; the 200-vehicle fleet figure is company-stated. Capacity and registration figures from the [Tesla SEC exhibit, 1 Aug 2026, Tier 1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1318605/000162828026003837/exhibit991.htm), `confirmed`.

### 2. Figure committed over $6 billion of compute to train humanoid robots

**What happened.** Figure and Nscale signed a multi-year partnership with an initial commitment of $3.5 billion in compute, scaling to more than $6 billion, covering up to 100,000 Nvidia Vera Rubin GPUs, according to the companies' announcement. Initial deployment is targeted for the second half of 2027 in Barstow, Texas. Nscale becomes a Figure shareholder and its preferred compute provider. The compute trains Figure's Helix robot foundation models.

**Why it matters.** Until today the humanoid sector's capital intensity sat in hardware and factories. This moves it into training compute at a scale previously reserved for language model labs, and it implies Figure believes robot policy learning follows a scaling curve steep enough to justify $6 billion before it has shipped meaningful unit volume. That is a testable claim: if Helix does not show measured generalization gains proportional to the compute, this becomes the sector's first stranded training commitment. It also puts 100,000 GPUs of load into the same Texas interconnection queue that hyperscalers are already sitting in.

**So what for me.** Nscale now has two disclosed anchor customers in a week's window, which makes the neocloud layer, not the robot, the more interesting position. For interviews at the labs, the sharper question is whether robot foundation models scale on compute or on teleoperation data hours, because the answer determines whether the constraint is GPUs or human demonstrators.

**Go deeper.** Figure's Helix technical posts are the primary material, roughly 25 minutes. Question: has Figure ever published a generalization curve against training compute, or only task demonstrations? Without the curve, the $6 billion rests on an unpublished belief.

**Second-order read, who loses.** Smaller humanoid entrants and the Chinese cost-down players. If the frontier of robot policy requires nine-figure training runs, Unitree-class pricing wins the hardware and loses the brain, and the value migrates to whoever owns the model.

**Source.** [Nscale and Figure announcement, PR Newswire, 3 Sep 2026, Tier 1](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/nscale-and-figure-sign-strategic-partnership-to-power-the-next-generation-of-physical-ai-302868918.html), `confirmed` as a company statement. Dollar figures are the companies' own and are not broken into contracted versus optional.

### 3. Two independent evaluators scored Astra and disagreed about what it is

**What happened.** ARC Prize published results for OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra: 62.7% on the ARC-AGI-3 semi-private set under ARC Prize's standard harness at a run cost of $26,098, and 99.9% under a provider-adapter harness that preserves opaque reasoning state between requests, at $18,817. Astra used 51.7% fewer actions per level than the human baseline. Separately, Artificial Analysis scored Astra at 61 on its Intelligence Index against 66 for Claude Fable 5.1, and 67 on its Coding Agent Index against 70. Astra uses roughly one third the tokens of GPT-5.6 Sol on the coding index but is priced at $10 and $50 per million input and output tokens, 2.5 times its predecessor.

**Why it matters.** The 37-point harness spread is the story. A model that scores 62.7% one way and 99.9% another is not being measured; the harness is. Anyone underwriting inference spend against a published benchmark is buying a number that depends on plumbing the vendor controls. The pricing result cuts the other way from the capability result: Astra is more token-efficient and still 75% more expensive per task on intelligence evals, because the price increase outran the efficiency gain. That is a vendor testing whether capability buys pricing power.

**So what for me.** Directly relevant to the monetization PM track. The Artificial Analysis finding is the interview-ready version: token efficiency and cost per task are different metrics, and a model can improve one while worsening the other. For the portfolio it argues that inference demand growth is not a straight function of token volume.

**Go deeper.** The ARC Prize results page, 20 minutes. Question: does the provider-adapter harness give Astra information the standard harness withholds, or does it only remove a re-serialization penalty? If the former, 99.9% is a different task.

**Second-order read, who loses.** Every leaderboard that reports one number per model, and every buyer who priced a contract off one. Artificial Analysis and ARC Prize publishing conflicting reads on the same model in two days is the useful outcome.

**Source.** [ARC Prize, 2 Sep 2026, Tier 1](https://arcprize.org/results/openai-gpt-6-astra) and [Artificial Analysis, 3 Sep 2026, Tier 2](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/benchmarking-gpt-6-astra), both `confirmed`. OpenAI's own Astra pages returned 403 on repeated attempts, so no vendor primary document backs these figures.

### 4. The G20 endorsed the US-drafted Carolina Principles

**What happened.** The G20 Innovation Ministerial concluded 2 September in Chapel Hill, hosted by Commerce and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, with a consensus statement covering six pillars plus the Carolina Principles for Emerging Technologies, the AI Prosperity Objectives, and an AI Prosperity Compact, according to the White House release. All represented G20 members took part, including China, Russia, the EU, India and Saudi Arabia, along with the African Union. The document does not itemize which countries adopted the principles as opposed to joining the consensus.

**Why it matters.** A consensus statement that includes both China and the EU is either a genuine floor for AI governance or a text weak enough that nobody had to concede. The distinction is legible in one place: whether the Carolina Principles create any obligation that conflicts with the EU AI Act's risk tiers. If they do not, this is a US-authored framing exercise that displaces the EU's claim to write the global default, which is the actual prize.

**So what for me.** Sovereign AI programs and export-control regimes are the policy layer under the infrastructure thesis. A US-drafted framework with Chinese and Gulf sign-on lowers the odds of a fragmented compliance regime that would raise the cost of deploying the same hardware across regions.

**Go deeper.** The consensus statement itself, 20 minutes. Question: does the text contain a single binding verb, or only "recognize," "encourage," and "commit to work toward"? Count them.

**Second-order read, who loses.** Brussels. The EU spent three years making the AI Act the global template on the Brussels-effect theory, and a consensus text with a different name and a US author is the first evidence that the theory is not automatic.

**Source.** [White House, 2 Sep 2026, Tier 1](https://www.whitehouse.gov/releases/2026/09/g20-innovation-ministerial-concludes-with-consensus-statement/), `confirmed`.

## Disconfirming evidence

Silicon Data's token expenditure index fell from a May peak of $2.0651 per million tokens to $0.9665 on 31 August, down 53%, against roughly $600 billion to $650 billion of 2026 hyperscaler AI capex. Artificial Analysis found the same pattern from the other direction: Astra needs one third the tokens of its predecessor on coding work. If price per token halves and tokens per task fall by two thirds, revenue per unit of deployed compute is falling on both axes at once while the capex commitments are fixed and debt-funded. The counter is that cheaper inference expands volume faster than price falls, which is the Jevons argument, and it may be right. It has not been demonstrated with a published number. Caveat on the source: the index figures come from a Tier 3 outlet relaying Silicon Data, not from the index publisher directly, which is worth confirming before leaning on it.

ARC Prize's GPT-6 Astra results page, 25 minutes. The answer determines which of the two numbers a buyer of inference capacity should underwrite, and right now vendors are free to quote either one.
