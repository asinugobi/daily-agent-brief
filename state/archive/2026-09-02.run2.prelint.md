## Today's vocabulary

**Term of the day: compute demand denominated in gigawatts.** Broadcom described its custom-accelerator pipeline in gigawatts of deployed capacity per customer, not in units or wafers. A gigawatt of AI capacity is roughly the output of one large nuclear reactor, and it bundles chips, racks, cooling, and interconnect into one number. The reason vendors quote it: power procurement and grid interconnection now set the delivery schedule, so the gigawatt figure tells you when revenue can actually land, while a chip count does not. In an argument, it is the bridge between a semiconductor forecast and a utility's capacity queue.

**Word of the day: XPU.** Broadcom's label for a customer-designed accelerator built to that customer's specification, as opposed to a merchant GPU sold to anyone. Broadcom said on the call that its FY27 and FY28 forecasts rest on six named XPU customers. The distinction matters because every XPU gigawatt is a gigawatt that a merchant GPU vendor does not sell.

## Executive summary

Broadcom's quarter is the cleanest read yet on how much of AI compute spending is moving to custom silicon. AI semiconductor revenue tripled to $16.7 billion, the company raised full-year AI revenue guidance to $58 billion from $56 billion, and it extended its Google TPU agreement. The forward numbers management gave on the call, $115 billion in FY27 and $230 billion in FY28, imply a buildout larger than most 2026 street models carry, and management framed the demand in gigawatts tied to named customers. That framing is the connective tissue with the power thesis: the ceiling on these forecasts is interconnection and generation, not fabs. Two things cut the other way. Total revenue guidance of $34.8 billion landed just below the roughly $35.03 billion consensus, which says the bar for AI-levered names is now high enough that tripling a revenue line is not automatically enough. And the custom-accelerator ramp is a share shift, which is a problem for a portfolio concentrated in merchant GPU exposure. Separately, OpenAI became the first lab to self-classify a model at the top cyber risk tier and delay release, which is the first real test of whether a capability threshold in a safety framework actually gates a product.

## Need to know

### 1. Broadcom's AI revenue tripled, and the forward guide is denominated in customer gigawatts

**What happened.** Broadcom reported Q3 FY26 revenue of $29.6 billion, up 86% year over year, with AI semiconductor revenue of $16.7 billion, up 221% year over year and 54% sequentially, according to the company's 8-K exhibit filed with the SEC. Non-GAAP operating income was $20.1 billion, a 68% margin, and free cash flow was $13.7 billion, 46% of revenue. Q4 guidance is $34.8 billion in total revenue and $21.7 billion in AI semiconductor revenue, up 236% year over year, with full-year FY26 AI revenue raised to $58 billion from a prior $56 billion. The filing says the Google TPU agreement was extended to deliver "multi tens of billions of dollars" of TPUs annually over the next several years.

**Why it matters.** The mechanism is substitution. Every gigawatt a hyperscaler fills with a Broadcom-built XPU is a gigawatt it does not fill with a merchant GPU, and Broadcom now has six named customers doing exactly that. On the call, management reportedly detailed the pipeline customer by customer: Google on TPU v7 and v8i, Anthropic at 1 GW of Ironwood in 2026 and 5 GW of TPU v8i in 2027, OpenAI shipping a custom accelerator with 1.3 GW in 2027, and Meta running three MTIA generations through 2027. For the FY27 figure of $115 billion to hold, those gigawatts have to get energized on schedule, which makes the forecast a bet on grid interconnection as much as on silicon.

**So what for me.** This is the strongest evidence to date for the convergence thesis, because a semiconductor vendor is now publishing its revenue forecast in units of power. It also argues against sizing AI compute exposure through a single merchant-GPU position: the custom-ASIC complex is capturing a growing share of the same buildout. For Forward Deployed and monetization PM interviews at the labs, the Anthropic and OpenAI accelerator lines are the concrete detail that shows you read the primary document rather than the headline.

**Go deeper.** The 8-K exhibit is about ten minutes. Hold one question: which of the forward-year figures are contracted backlog with delivery schedules, and which are customer demand forecasts that can be revised? The 8-K supports the FY26 numbers. It does not, on its own, support the FY27 and FY28 figures.

**Second-order read, who loses.** Merchant GPU pricing power. If six customers can each stand up multi-gigawatt custom fleets, the marginal buyer of a general-purpose accelerator has a credible alternative for inference workloads, which is where the volume is.

### 2. OpenAI put a model in the top cyber risk tier and held it back

**What happened.** OpenAI classified Astra as "Critical" for cybersecurity capability under its Preparedness Framework, the first model to reach that tier, SecurityWeek reported. The classification means the model can independently find and exploit previously unknown vulnerabilities across well-defended systems. During evaluation it discovered two zero-day vulnerabilities, broke out of a browser sandbox to run commands on the host machine, and chained operating system flaws to reach root access. It declined 91.5% of cyber-related jailbreak attempts, against 59% for its predecessor. OpenAI said further safeguards are required, limited initial access to a testing group, and routed broader availability through a program it calls Daybreak Blue.

**Why it matters.** Every frontier lab publishes a safety framework with capability thresholds, and until now no threshold had ever bound. This one did, and it produced a delayed release and a gated rollout. That converts the frameworks from disclosure documents into something closer to a product gate, which changes launch timing risk for every lab that has committed to one. The commercial read runs the other way: a model that can find zero-days is directly monetizable in offensive and defensive security, and gated access is a pricing structure as much as a safety control.

**So what for me.** This is a live case study for the monetization PM track, where the question is how you price and gate a capability you have publicly called dangerous. It also sets up the enterprise AI security trade, since a model at this capability level raises the value of the defensive tooling that CrowdStrike and others are shipping.

**Go deeper.** The system card for Astra, when OpenAI publishes it, is the document. It does not appear to be out yet. Until it is, the claim rests on one outlet. The question to hold: does the Preparedness Framework specify who approves crossing a Critical threshold, or does the lab decide case by case?

**Source.** [SecurityWeek, 2 Sep 2026, Tier 2](https://www.securityweek.com/openais-astra-becomes-first-model-to-cross-critical-cybersecurity-threshold/) — `reported`. Single source. No OpenAI primary document was located in this run.

### 3. Waymo added three cities and passed 4,000 vehicles, two days before Tesla's Cybercab event

**What happened.** Waymo launched robotaxi service in Denver, San Diego, and Tampa, bringing it to 14 US cities, TechCrunch reported. The fleet now exceeds 4,000 vehicles, about 300 of them Ojai minivans and the rest Jaguar I-Pace. Nevada has approved up to 1,000 vehicles over the next year for a Las Vegas launch, and London and Munich are planned. Waymo says it runs about 500,000 paid trips a week and has logged more than 200 million autonomous miles. Tesla formally introduces Cybercab on 3 September with a stated production target above 125,000 units a year, and has been registering vehicles with the Texas DMV.

**Why it matters.** The two companies are now testing opposite constraints. Waymo's is unit cost and city-by-city permitting, with roughly 125 paid trips per vehicle per week implied by its own numbers. Tesla's is regulatory, because a 125,000-unit annual target only means anything if the vehicles are approved to operate without a safety driver at that scale. Whichever constraint binds first determines which cost curve gets to matter.

**So what for me.** Fleet electrification is the second leg of the convergence thesis, and 4,000 vehicles across 14 cities is still small enough that AV charging load does not yet move a utility forecast. Worth tracking as a leading indicator rather than a position.

**Go deeper.** Waymo's own safety and mileage disclosures are the primary source; the trip and mileage figures here are company-reported and passed through a Tier 3 outlet. Twenty minutes. Question: is trips per vehicle per week rising, or is Waymo growing volume purely by adding vehicles?

**Source.** [TechCrunch, 1 Sep 2026, Tier 3](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/01/waymo-accelerates-robotaxi-expansion-with-launches-in-denver-san-diego-and-tampa/) and [TechCrunch, 1 Sep 2026, Tier 3](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/01/waymo-goes-on-offense-ahead-of-teslas-cybercab-launch/) — `reported`.
